What is a serial link?

A serial link is the system that establishes communication between two integrated circuits — everything it takes to move data reliably from one chip to another.

/The idea

Strip away the details and a serial link is a simple concept: one integrated circuit has data, another one needs it, and the serial link is the complete system in between — transmitter, physical channel, and receiver working together to deliver that data intact.

Chip A→ Copper or optical channel→ Chip B

/Copper or light

The channel — the physical medium carrying the signal — comes in two flavors:

  • Copper: a cable or a PCB trace. Cheap and ubiquitous, but lossy at high frequencies.
  • Optical: fiber, with electrical-to-optical conversion at each end. Far lower loss over distance, at the cost of extra complexity.

Either way, the engineering challenge is the same: push more bits per second through an imperfect physical medium.

/The real problem: speed

The dominant trend in serial links is ever-increasing speed — and every speed jump brings a new set of problems. Higher data rates mean the channel attenuates the signal more, bits smear into their neighbors (inter-symbol interference), crosstalk and jitter eat into timing margins, and power consumption climbs.

Solving these problems is precisely what serial-link engineers do: equalization to undo channel loss, clock recovery to extract timing from the data itself, and careful analog design to keep everything working at tens of gigabaud.

Key idea: a serial link is never "just a wire" — it is a full system engineered around the physics of moving bits faster with every generation.

/State of the art

Today, the state of the art in serial links is transmitting at a data rate of 200 gigabits per second per lane — and 400-gigabit-per-lane links over copper are already in development. Each doubling reopens every problem above, which is why this field never stands still.
