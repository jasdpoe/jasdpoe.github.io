What is a SerDes?

A SerDes — serializer/deserializer — is the pair of circuits that lets a handful of pins move hundreds of gigabits per second. It sits at the heart of PCIe, USB, Ethernet, and most chip-to-chip links.

/Why serialize?

Moving data in parallel is simple: one wire per bit, all switching together. But parallel buses don't scale. Every extra pin costs package area, every trace needs length matching, and at gigahertz speeds, skew and crosstalk between parallel lines become unmanageable.

Serialization trades width for speed: take N parallel bits, send them one after another over a single differential pair, and reconstruct them on the other side. Fewer pins, simpler routing, and the link can run at tens of gigabaud per lane.

/The signal chain

A complete SerDes link looks like this:

Serializer→ TX driver + FFE→ Channel→ CTLE→ DFE→ CDR→ Deserializer
  • Serializer: converts the parallel word into a serial bit stream, usually with a high-speed multiplexer tree.
  • TX driver + FFE: launches the signal into the channel. A feed-forward equalizer (FFE) pre-emphasizes transitions to pre-compensate the channel's high-frequency loss.
  • Channel: PCB traces, vias, connectors. It behaves like a low-pass filter, smearing each bit into its neighbors — inter-symbol interference (ISI).
  • CTLE: a continuous-time linear equalizer at the receiver that provides high-frequency peaking to undo part of the channel loss.
  • DFE: a decision-feedback equalizer that subtracts the ISI contributed by previously decided bits — no noise amplification, unlike linear equalization.
  • CDR: the clock-and-data recovery loop extracts timing from the data itself, since no separate clock is sent. It must lock to the incoming phase and track slow drift.
  • Deserializer: converts the recovered serial stream back to parallel words for the digital core.
Key idea: a SerDes is a mixed-signal control problem. The analog front end fights the physics of the channel, while adaptation loops (for CTLE peaking, DFE taps, CDR phase) continuously optimize the link in the background.

/Where you'll find them

Everywhere high-speed data moves: PCIe lanes between CPU and GPU, USB4/Thunderbolt cables, 112G Ethernet switch fabrics, memory interfaces, and die-to-die links inside multi-chip packages. The data rates keep climbing — 224 Gb/s per lane is on the horizon — which is why equalization and CDR design remain such an active area.
